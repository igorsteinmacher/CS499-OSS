Eric S. Raymond presented The Cathedral and the Bazaar at a Linux conference in 1997
and later expanded it into a book. After studying how Linus Torvalds ran Linux, he wrote his
own program fetchmail through using the same approach. The essay is credited with convincing
Netscape to release its browser code in 1998, and its terms are still widely used today. The
cathedral is Raymond's name for the traditional way of building software. Like a real cathedral, a
small group of experts works from a master plan, in private, and nobody outside sees the work
until it is finished. Early free software projects like GNU Emacs and GCC were built this way,
because a small core team controlled every decision even though anyone could read the code.
The cathedral has several features. A small, closed team designs and builds the whole
system. Bugs are treated as hard problems that take months of study by a few specialists in order
to solve. Users receive the finished product but do not help create it. Decisions come from the
top down through a central plan. The belief behind this is that quality comes from control: fewer
people, tighter reviews, and longer gaps between releases mean fewer mistakes.
The bazaar is Raymond's name for the open, community driven approach he saw in
Linux. The picture is a marketplace full of independent traders with their own goals, out of
which a working system appears. Raymond had previously expected the Linux approach to fall
apart, and much of the essay explains why it did not. In the Bazaar, development was open to
anyone, so the number of people writing and testing code is large and always changing. New
versions are released early and often, even when the code is not perfect. Users are treated as co-
developers who report bugs, suggest fixes, and test newly released changes who’s input actually
goes into future iterations. Bugs are seen as shallow, because once enough people look at a
problem, someone is almost assured to find it. The project leader picks the best contributions
rather than dictating every design choice and leads through persuasion and reputation instead of
authority.
Raymond sums up what he learned in nineteen lessons. Every good program starts with a
developer solving a problem they personally have. Good programmers know what to write; great
ones know what to reuse. Plan to throw the first version away. With the right attitude, interesting
problems will come to you. When you lose interest in a program, hand it to a capable successor.
Treating users as co-developers is the fastest way to improve and debug a program. Release early
and often and listen to users. With enough testers, nearly every bug is quickly found and fixed by
someone, which he calls Linus's Law. Smart data structures with simple code beat the reverse. If
you treat testers as your most valuable resource, they will become that. Recognizing a good idea
from a user is the next best thing to having one. The most creative solutions often come from
realizing you misunderstood the problem. A design is perfect when there is nothing left to take
away. A great tool gets used in ways its maker never expected. Programs that pass data along
should change it as little as possible. In a simple language, a little syntactic sugar helps. A
security system is only as secure as its secret, fake secrets are also dangerous. To solve an
interesting problem, start with one that interests you. Finally, as long as the leader has a
communication tool as good as the Internet and can lead without force, many heads are better
than one.
The Cathedral and the Bazaar have lasted so long because they explain something the
software world had noticed but not fully understood: openness, fast iteration, and wide
participation can beat central planning. The bazaar still needs skilled leaders, steady funding, and
good tools, and although some lessons apply mostly to small programs, almost everything in the
paper is still applicable today and its main ideas have been so absorbed into agile, DevOps, and
open-source work that they now feel like common sense.
